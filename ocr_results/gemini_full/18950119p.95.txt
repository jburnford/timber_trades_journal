AN American journal, in giving a portrait of Mr. David Ferris, manager of the Oregon and California Lumber Company, of Portland, Ore., says: "Mr. Ferris is in every sense a pioneer. He came to the Pacific coast in 1864, and since then he has been actively engaged in some branch of the lumber business. He made lumber his profession and knows his business in all its details. His experience is of that practical character that is not easily forgotten, and a reminiscence carries him back to the time when he fired in a saw-mill, logged in the woods, kept books in a lumber office, sold the stuff on the road, and ran a number of successful retail yards, his last connection with the retail trade being in El Paso, Texas, where he conducted a yard for two years, leaving there in 1889 to take the management of the Portland concern. Mr. Ferris was born in Scotland. When a child, moved to Canada when he was four years old, and immediately after to Buffalo, N.Y., where young Ferris had the benefit of a liberal education. Like all lumbermen, he is excessively modest, and considers the camera an instrument of torture, consequently he has 'smiled and looked pleasant' only once during his whole life. He is an enthusiastic Hoo Hoo—one of the first in Oregon—and considers a concatenation a terrestrial paradise."

DURABILITY OF WOODEN SLEEPERS.

The last statement published by the Public Works Department of India shows the results of the experiments to test the durability of wooden railway sleepers up to the end of the year 1892.

On another section of this line, out of 13,042 creosoted pine sleepers laid down in 1880 only about 20 per cent. are still sound, and out of 450 deodar on the same section only 29 per cent. On the Rewari-Ferozpore section, of 1,166 deodar laid down in 1882 68 per cent. are still sound, whereas of a small number of creosoted pine 84 per cent. were removed after four years.
Again, in another part of the same section, out of 11,943 deodar laid down in 1884 only 74 per cent. have so far been removed, but the time is rather short for this result to be of much value.
On the North-Western Railway out of 1,416 deodar laid down in 1877 79 per cent. are still sound after fifteen years, whereas of 1,144 creosoted pine out of the same experiment none remain, the average age of this kind of sleeper at the time of removal being thirteen years.
The results obtained on this railway are, however, a little variable, as in another portion laid down in 1878 were all removed in thirteen years, whereas the deodar seems to have fared better, as out of 382 only 193 had to be removed after fourteen years; the number of the latter in this case, however, is rather too small to affect the general results of the experiments, which still remain, as heretofore, in favour of deodar.
On the Southern Mahratta Railway out of a small number of teak and creosoted pine laid down in 1880 93 per cent. of the former and 60 per cent. of the latter are still sound. In another part of the same line out of 1,020 teak and 589 pine (creosoted) 91 per cent. and 55 per cent. respectively are still sound after twelve years.
On the Eastern Bengal Railway, in the experiment of 1877, out of 1,973 sal 59 per cent. are still sound, whereas of 1,923 creosoted pine, only 9 per cent. still remain.
In another portion of the same line sal shows 73 per cent. still sound out of 2,006 laid down in 1879.
The other experiments on this line extend over shorter periods, but it may be noted that 57 of the 1,823 iron-wood sleepers laid down in 1884 had to be removed during the year 1892.
