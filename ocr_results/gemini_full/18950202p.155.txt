FEBRUARY 2, 1895.

FORESTRY LECTURES.

Mr. Slater, of Haystoun, Peebles, has delivered his second lecture on "The Valuation of Woodlands, and the Disposal of their Produce, with its Transport, Conversion, Qualities, and Uses," in the Royal Botanic Gardens.
In addition to the students, there were present Professor Balfour, Colonel Bailey, R.E., Messrs. Moffat, Galloway, Annan, Welsh, Massie, Milne, &c.
The lecturer entered into the various modes for the disposal of woodland produce, and pointed out the methods that were likely to meet the best results in localities where the markets and means of transport were referred to the condition of the various modes of disposal, and pointed out that these, while safeguarding the interests of the disposer, should be simple and liberal in their provisions.
In referring to wind-blown timber, the lecturer condemned the policy pursued by many of those who were so unfortunate in having their woodlands devastated by storms in hastily rushing all their wood on the market, thus causing a glut which seriously affects others who were in a more fortunate position, but whose sales from their periodical sales were seriously reduced.
He went on to show that instead of that policy, the wood were seasoned and allowed to go off gradually, the market would remain steadier, with the result that better prices would be obtained all round.
The private conversion of timber was considered, but in the present condition of forestry, it was, he said, better left to those legitimately in the trade.
With reference to the transport of timber, notice was taken of its weight, and the shrinkage to be obtained by removing the bark, and allowing it to partially dry before it was transported.
By the aid of diagrams of the different vehicles, it was explained how they were moved and loaded, and the weights that may be carried on each.
The lecture was concluded by reference to the motors necessary for the conversion of timber, such as the various classes of water-wheels, capable of the turbine, and the efficiencies of these were given.
Steam, gas, and oil-engines were also noticed; and in referring to the latter, mention was made of the improvement effected on them during late years, and that, as they were self-contained, they could readily be moved from place to place.
At the close of the lecture Professor Balfour, in proposing a vote of thanks to Mr. Slater, said that, owing to another engagement, he would be unable to be present at the next lecture; but he might say now what he had intended to say then, that there was little use in teaching them how to grow trees if they did not know how to treat them afterwards.
They were, therefore, much indebted for these lectures; but as they had, no doubt, got more than they could digest, Mr. Slater had consented that they should be printed, and he, the Professor, would have that done, and hand each student a copy.

NAPTHALINE.—On one or two of the larger estates in the south of Scotland the use of "Napthaline" as a medium for preserving wood paving has been largely employed.
The posts, or "stobs" as they are locally called, are steeped in a bath of napthaline heated to 180 deg. or 200 deg. until they are thoroughly permeated, and the difference in hardness between a "dipped" and "undipped" material is at once apparent in weight and resistance to a knife.
The advocates of the process have so far not had full time to demonstrate its usefulness; but, whereas an ordinary larch or fir "stob" becomes almost useless after an exposure in from five to six years, a "dipped" post was shown which had been in the ground for that period, and was as sound as when it was first put in.
Spite of the line is the refuse obtained from the paraffin oil works, of which there are many in the land, and costs little more than its cartage, so that the cost of dipping and material is liberally placed at one penny, an infinitesimal sum as compared to the results which are claimed to be achieved.
In any case, the utilisation of what would otherwise be a waste is worth a trial.

THE OBAN CAVE.
A meeting of the Glasgow Archaeological Society was held in the society's rooms, Bath Street, Glasgow, when a paper, written by Mr. W. Anderson Smith, was read, giving particulars of the recent discovery of an old cave at Oban.
Mr. C. D. Donald, president of the society, occupied the chair, and there was a large attendance.
After describing the contents of the cave, the author proceeded:— Enough has been found to justify a more careful and reliable examination, not only of what remains in situ, but of what material has been removed and not sufficiently sifted.
The finding of good troglodyte skulls is of much importance, considering the non-Celtic character and yet good type.
The elegant make of the bone harpoons at the lowest level is evidence of a certain advance in structural skill.
The bones of deer would suggest a hunting as well as a fishing existence.
Yet domestic animals are also present, unless the oxen and pigs are wild.
But the bones must pass through the hands of an expert ere any theory can be based on them, and any negative evidence, in the absence of a very thorough examination of all the material, must count for little.
No prehistoric find in the West of Scotland promises more fruity results, and the readiness with which the proprietor has met those interested presents a rare opportunity for examining the whole with accuracy.
The exposure of the whole floor, by the necessary removal of the shaky rock, demands prompt action to save the material.
Mr. W. G. Black, secretary of the society, added that since the paper was written the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland had agreed to make a grant towards the proper investigation of the cave.

BUILDING TRADE IN ARBROATH IN 1894.—The past year has been a busy year in the Arbroath building trade, mainly owing to the erection of the new High School.
There have also been a good many masons employed at the restoration of the Old Church.
There have also been a good many buildings of the ordinary type erected in the course of the year.

PROPOSED NEW BRIDGE AT THE DOCK GATES.—Mr. William Smith, Harbour Engineer, has prepared two plans showing a scheme for the erection of a bridge at the Dock Gates to take the place of the present old structure.
One proposal is to construct a steel girder bridge to work in the existing masonry recesses, with a carriageway of thirteen feet and a raised pathway of six feet.
The alternative design shows a single steel girder bridge spanning both entrances.
The roadway would be fifteen feet wide and the footpath on each side five feet wide.
The bridge would be swung upon a single cylinder on the island on a hydraulic pivot, and, in addition to carriage traffic, would, if necessary, carry railway traffic.
The schemes, either of which would cost about £4,000, will presently come before the Works Committee of the Harbour Board.

INVERNESS v. ABERDEEN.
Members of the Inverness Chamber of Commerce have been discussing the rival schemes of railway extension in the Highlands, Bills for which will come before Parliament in the ensuing session.
The Chamber is opposed to the Great North of Scotland Railway Company obtaining running powers over the Highland Railway, the chief argument being that such powers would lead to the development of Aberdeen to the detriment of Inverness.
The Chamber also debated at great length the merits of the plans of the Highland and West Highland Land Companies for the construction of a line between Inverness and Fort-William, and the majority resolved to petition in favour of the Bill of the Highland Company, and thus follow the example set by the Inverness Town Council.

ARBROATH.—A conference has taken place between a committee of the Town Council of Arbroath; Mr. Hamilton, manager of the Dundee and Arbroath Joint Line; Mr. Barr, engineer to the Caledonian Railway Company; and Mr. Bell, assistant engineer to the North British Railway Company, on the proposals for the construction of the station at Arbroath.
The plans were before the committee, and explanations on a number of points were given by the engineers.
The committee of the Town Council expressed their general approval of the plans, and agreed to recommend the Town Council to accept them.

ABERDEEN.—At a meeting of the Finance Committee of Aberdeen Town Council it was reported that, according to instructions the committee had purchased from the ground formerly owned by the Council for the sum of between £2,000 and £3,000.
The question of acquiring ground was remitted to a small committee.
A communication was submitted from the Town Clerk of Glasgow with reference to the position of Town Councils in Scotland in regard to the Postmaster-General making application to the Postmaster-General for a licence to construct a telephonic system, and asking that a representative from the Council of Aberdeen should be appointed to join a deputation of the municipalities of Scotland in order to lay the matter before the Postmaster-General.
It was decided that Treasurer Bisset be appointed the delegate from Aberdeen.
