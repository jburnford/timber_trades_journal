FEBRUARY 9, 1895.
THE FORESTRY AND [?]
EDITORIAL NOTES.
Large Beech Trees.
From a letter which it will be seen that one of the largest beech trees in Scotland was uprooted during the severe and long-continued storm of December last. The tree is remembered to contain fully two thousand feet of timber—contents that are certainly not surpassed, if even equalled, by any other specimen growing in the British Isles. The famous Knole beech, although of unusually large stem girth, does not contain a proportionate quantity of timber, the trunk being short and ramifying into large limbs. We measured this tree some years ago, with the present owner of the property, Lord Sackville, when we made the dimensions then being:—Girth of stem at 3 ft., 32 ft.; girth of stem at 5 ft., 31 ft. 7 in.; diameter of branch spread, 96 ft. In Loudon's time the tree was computed to contain 1,098 ft. of timber and was 85 ft. high. At Holwood Park, in Kent, there is a beautiful specimen of the beech, girthing 20 ft. 6 in. at a yard from the ground, and containing 295 ft. of measurable timber. The largest and cleanest stem of beech that we have seen is that growing at Woburn Abbey, in Bedfordshire, the exact measurements of which were, by the wish of one of the Dukes of Bedford, recorded in the “Arboretum et fruticetum Britannicum.” It is in excellent health, and growing rapidly, with a huge, well-rounded bole that is clear of branches for about 60 ft. in height.

The Chupress Arborvitæ (Thuja orientalis).
Long experience has proved this to be one of the most useful and accommodating of garden shrubs, for in this country it is hardly entitled to the name of tree, few specimens having attained to the specified 20 ft. in height. There are many varieties—some worthy, others not so—but that of pendulous growth named T. orientalis pendula is certainly one of the most distinct and ornamental. So distinct indeed is it from the typical plant that it has long been favoured with specific recognition, but it is now known to have originated from the common form, cuttings having been produced in various parts of the country, which sufficiently settle its identity. There is a beautifully-developed specimen growing at Esher, in Surrey, in which the long, pendulous, cord-like branch appendages are well developed. It is bearing quantities of fruit which in every way resemble those of T. orientalis, thus settling its disputed right to specific identity. For ornamental planting it is well worthy of attention.

Damage to Forest Trees by the Vole and Field Mouse.
We have of late had numerous inquiries and damaged specimens of young trees forwarded regarding the destruction of the vole and field mouse, both of which would seem to be unusually plentiful and to have committed much damage during the past summer and autumn. Some years ago, when a paper was read on the ravages committed in young plantations on an estate controlled by these vermin, the writer stating that he had to be dug in the woods for them, and so partially arrest the destruction. In several large plantations near the sea coast, constant vigilance to be a source of considerable expense in keeping the pests at bay or destroying them. Young ash, oak, and other trees, from the attacks made on the bark of the leading shoots of rats, had withered and died down to their roots.
