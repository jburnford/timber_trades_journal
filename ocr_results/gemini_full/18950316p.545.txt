THE PROTECTION OF FENCING.
IT is a grave but oft-committed mistake
to attempt to render fencing materials more
lasting by coating the timber with tar, creo-
sote, petroleum, or any of the often-
recommended specifics, without first having
the wood thoroughly dry and well seasoned.
This applies with greatest force when the
application is simply used as a coating, and
less so when the pores of the wood are fully
impregnated with the composition.
Several attempts have been made on
estates throughout the country to preserve
theseasotted timber by dipping or coating it
over with tar for creosote, but almost
every instance have been attended with
very unsatisfactory results, the reason
being that while the outside was preserved the
imprisoned natural sap soon caused the inner wood to
pent-up and rapidly decay. Far better never to tar or creosote
timber outwardly than to do so when the
wood is wet, sappy, and unseasoned, and a
case that came under our notice a few days
ago of an expensive park-screen falling
to pieces in a few years owing to being
tarred when the wood was green formed a
good object lesson. The fence in question,
which was formed of 6 in. wide pine planking,
the pales overlapping each other by fully an
inch, the posts being also of pine wood, both
these and the pales having been cut from
heavy and matured home-grown Scotch fir.
Whether, owing to thoughtlessness on
the part of the estate fencers or to ignorance,
the fence immediately after being erected
was thickly coated with tar, this process being
repeated every second year, more, perhaps,
for appearance sake than for the lasting
properties of the fence. In eight years after
being erected the shaky condition of the
fence pointed out that it was not so stable as
could have been desired; and in another two
years many of the posts had given way not
only at ground level, but at points higher up
while the edges of fully one-fourth of the
pales were noticeably commencing to crumble
away.
Before applying anything to prevent rot,
timber should first be thoroughly seasoned,
for if the natural sap is still in the timber it
will prevent perfect absorption, and this
will prevent the escape of the sap, which will
soon ferment and cause premature and
immediate decay. When fresh felled or
unseasoned fencing posts must be used we
have found it an excellent plan to char
slowly the end of each, and for a foot
higher than will be buried, and then to
apply the preservative composition. This
is a simple and inexpensive process, and
consists in making a fire of logs and
chips on the ground, and so getting a
red mass of charred wood without much
blaze. The ends of the posts are held over
this until they cease to give off vapour, and
are then, whilst warm, either dipped or
painted over with tar or creosote. This
simple process will have the effect of
enhancing the lasting properties of the
wood for many years and does not
cost, including tarring, one-halfpenny
extra per post. A point of much importance
is to char the post for not less than 6 in.
above the depth it is intended to be placed
in the ground. The pales or rails may be
added at any time to the fence, but should
never be painted with any composition until
they are thoroughly dry and seasoned.

REVIEW.
Housing the People. By Sir Hugh Gilzean-
Reid, J.P. Paisley and London:
Alexander Gardner.
This is an interesting history of a well-
known experiment in house-building in
Edinburgh, which has, according to the
author, much promise. In closing his intro-
ductory note he says:—" The movement
advances, growing and gathering strength;
and with the increased facilities for borrow-
ing money provided by a timely Act of
Parliament, the production of dwellings ought
to be taken up with systematic energy in
numerous towns and districts in which the
housing of the people is defective."

The following may also be planted with a
prospect of success: Aucuba japonica, azaleas
of sorts, berberries of sorts, dogwood, elder
of sorts, guelder rose, ivies of sorts, lilac of
sorts, pernettya of sorts, privet of sorts,
laurestinus, Skimmia japonica, box tree,
Daphne laureola, bay laurels, Portugal
laurels, yews, &c. In the tree tribe
we may mention the acacia or locust
tree, evergreen oak, maple of sorts,
Eastern and Western plane, sycamore, beech,
ash, elm, cherry, alder, weeping birch,
mountain ash, hornbeam, laburnum, pear
of sorts, willow of sorts, service tree, pear
and apple tree, thorns of sorts, lime tree, &c.
These are a few of the best, but in suburban
districts, where the smoke is less dense, a few
of the hardier conifers may be planted where
desirable as a mixture for the sake of con-
trast and variety. In order to attain
success in planting the first prelimi-
nary step to be taken in the prepara-
tion of the ground is to see that it is
thoroughly drained. Stagnant water at the
roots is not only injurious, but in many cases
actually kills the plants altogether. By
draining the ground the stagnant surface and
subsoil water is removed, the temperature of
the soil increased, and thus rendered friable
and fertile for the roots. It is also necessary
to have the ground trenched about 18 in.
deep; and in all cases where the subsoil con-
sists of hard till, or stiff, argillaceous clay,
such should be well broken up with a pick
in order to render it active and increase its
fertility. Another important matter in the
preparation of the ground is to have all
dead roots and decayed timber removed
in order to render it as pure and clean
as possible for the roots of the
plants. At places where dead trees have
been stubbed out of the ground, all chips,
dead wood, and rubbish should be collected
and burned, as, if mixed with the soil, they
encourage the growth of different species of
fungi, and as these often attack the roots of
healthy trees and shrubs, and cause their
death, all rational means ought to be used to
keep them within bounds. The study of the
diseases of trees and shrubs is essential to
their proper cultivation, and although we
can do but little to mitigate the evils result-
ing from a vitiated atmosphere, yet by
judicious and proper preparation of the soil
we can do much towards arresting and ward-
ing off disease which originates at the roots,
and thus encourage the healthy development
of the plants. In all cases where the soil is
of a stiff, adhesive nature lime may
be applied with advantage, as it
not only converts dormant matter into an
active state, and thus increases its fertility,
but also kills mycelium spawn of fungus
whose presence is not only injurious, but
often kills trees and shrubs outright. Such
soil would likewise be much improved by
being trenched in autumn, and left in a
rough state exposed to the ameliorating in-
fluence of frost during winter, by which
means it will be rendered loose and friable
for planting in spring. A proper selection of
plants is not only indispensable for different
soils and situations, but great care should be
exercised in lifting and removing them from
nursery ground, in order to prevent damage
to the roots, and in carrying out such work
I have found steel forks to be better adapted
for the work than spades, as they can be used
with greater facility than the latter, and less
risk of cutting and destroying roots and young
fibres.
It, however, sometimes happens that roots
get fractured in the course of lifting, and
when such is the case the bruised part
should be cut off with a sharp knife, by
placing the blade at the underside and
cutting upwards and outwards, thus leaving
a clean sloping surface to the wound. In
planting trees and shrubs great care should
be taken not to plant them too deep. As a
general rule, the roots should be spread out
in a regular manner from the collar of the
plant, and covered with soil of a loose, open
texture, and when finished they should be
the same depth in the soil as they were
previously to being lifted from the nursery.
It is also a matter of importance to
have young trees properly tied or
staked according to their requirement to
prevent windwaying. Trees of some
6 or 8 ft. high may be properly secured by
driving a wooden peg into the ground at the
base of the stem, say about 20 in. high above
the surface, and by placing a piece of
matting or other soft material around the
stem to prevent friction they can be tied and
secured in a very efficient manner. Trees
of such a size as the above are often staked,
5 ft. in length; but with poles of about
such a length is un-necessary, and in many
cases is injurious, as they present a greater
surface to the force of the wind, and are apt
to be blown to one side and thus defeat the
intended, whereas the peg keeps the plant
firm at the root and allows the stem to
oscillate with the wind, which is more
natural and much more tidy to appearance,
In order to prevent too sudden evaporation
from the soil, large plants will be benefited
by a mulching to keep the ground damp and
cool till such time as the roots take to
ground and establish themselves.

HOLYROOD ABBEY AND PALACE.—Mr.
W. W. Robertson, of H.M. Board of Works,
delivered a lecture on "Holyrood Abbey
and Palace," in Queen Street Hall, Edin-
burgh, under the auspices of the Photo-
graphic Society. Mr. Robertson described
(1) the ruined fragment of the old Abbey,
now known as the Chapel Royal; (2) the
great tower built by James IV. of the old
palace; and (3) the palace erected by
Charles II., representing the bulk of the
buildings. One great charm of the ancient
Abbey was, he said, that most of what was
to be seen was genuine old work. The on-
looker was face to face with the old builders.
Mr. Robertson next described the other
two portions of the buildings, and said their
surpassing interest lay in the way in which
they linked the Scotland of to-day with cen-
turies long past.
