SCOTTISH ARBORICULTURAL SOCIETY.
EXCURSION TO GERMANY.
Arrangements for the annual excursion are sufficiently advanced to enable the council to intimate that members taking part in the tour in Germany will sail from Leith by the s.s. "Prague" (s) on July 26th, arriving at Hamburg on the 28th.
Thereafter, eight days will be spent inspecting the interesting forests and plantations on the moors of Luneburg, the spruce woods in the Government forests of Hanover and in the Harz, and the pine forests of Scots firs and other conifers in the Odenwalde, the Forest Academy of Eisenach, and a large commercial nursery near Halberstadt.
It is also proposed to visit the old mining town of Goslar, famous for its ancient buildings; to ascend the Brocken, and to visit other interesting points in North Germany, and to spend a day in Berlin and Potsdam on the return journey will be made from Hamburg by steamer, arriving at Leith on August 8th.
It is estimated that the necessary expenses of the trip per member will be about £15.
As it is necessary to engage berths, carriages, rooms, &c., two months in advance, the council desire that members who propose to join the party shall send in their names, with a deposit of £1, to the secretary not later than May 15th, after which date applications can be entertained only in the event of vacancies occurring.

THE POSITION OF BO'NESS DOCKS.
A public meeting of the inhabitants of Bo'ness was held on Tuesday night to consider the proposed acquisition of the Dock by the North British Railway Company.
Provost Stewart, who presided, explained the offer which had been made by the railway company and the terms of the provisional arrangements come to between the town's representatives on the Harbour Board and the railway company.
So far as the burgh was concerned, he said, they had only an interest in the management of the dock, and that could never yield any return in £ s. d.
The property of the undertaking was vested in Harbour Commissioners, but seeing the harbour was so heavily mortgaged there was not the slightest chance of a reversionary interest accruing to the burgh at any time.
The first offer made by the railway company was to lease the dock from the Harbour Board and to pay the salaries of the officials.
This the burgh members could not agree to.
The next offer was to purchase the dock and pay for the town's rights therein £250 per annum, with the option to the railway company to buy up the annuity for a lump sum of £8,000.
This offer could not be accepted either.
The offer now made was to pay £250 per annum, rising by £50 every two years to £500, and with no option to the railway company to buy up this amount, which would be paid for all time.
This sum capitalised would be worth about £20,000 to the burgh.
The Commissioners had unanimously approved of this bargain, and he hoped it would also meet with the approval of the inhabitants.
In answer to questions, the Provost stated that the Railway Company stipulated that the right of way over the railway to the foreshore at Corbiehall should be included in the bargain, and if this right of way were given up the railway company were to erect an over-bridge giving access to the foreshore, and charge for its upkeep £10 yearly.
If the inhabitants wished to negotiate for the dock alone and to retain the right of way, then the annual payment would only rise to £200.
Mr. George Denholm, shipbroker, moved a resolution in favour of the offer being accepted, and this was agreed to unanimously.

EDINBURGH ARCHITECTURAL ASSOCIATION.
A meeting of the Edinburgh Architectural Association was held in the Royal Institution Mound, Mr. W. W. Robertson, the president, in the chair, Mr. John Kinross, A.R.S.A., made a communication on St. Andrew's Priory.
He gave it as his opinion that the evidence was amply sufficient to prove that the monastic buildings were commenced after the cathedral building was completed.
Mr. Thomas Bonnar read a communication on "Some Notes on the Ancient Mural Decorative Art in Scotland."
Dividing the subject into two divisions—the ecclesiastical and the secular periods—he said, of the first, that, owing to the ravages which left the ancient religious edifices of our country in a totally ruinous condition, there were only two examples known to him of ecclesiastical coloured work illustrating this phase of art in Scotland.
The more important specimen was, namely, the groined roof of the crypt of Glasgow Cathedral.
That remarkable and highly interesting piece of decorative work, although in a decayed condition, showed by its presence on the roof of that ancient structure—which dated back to the 12th century—that the interiors of religious houses were highly decorated with ornamental forms, and that the use of rich colouring was prevalent in those early days of the Church of Scotland.
The mullions that formed the groined arches of the roof were deeply moulded and filled in with full colouring of red, yellow, and white.
The centre bosses were also tinted with similar colours, and there was evidence that the enriched carvings had been overlaid with gold.
At the intersections of the groins and at the bases of the arches there were painted on the plastered stone arches richly twining ornamental scrolls representing conventional foliage, the prevailing tint being green.
The drawing of the ornament exhibited great precision, while it retained much spirit in the flow of its lines, proclaiming it to be the work of a proficient artist.
These particulars were noted many years ago, but he was surprised and disappointed to find, on a recent visit, that not one vestige of the ancient work now remained.
So thoroughly had it been obliterated that it was evident that the chisel had been employed to accomplish it—the worst act of Vandalism of recent times.
The other proof of colour being introduced on stone work was to be seen in Dryburg Abbey within the chapel adjoining the one where lies the greatest of the romancists, Sir Walter Scott.
Turning to the secular part of the subject, he said it was impossible to determine the probable date when this class of work was first used for interior decoration.

The earliest dated ceiling extant was the Nunraw one, the year inscribed on which was 1461.
Borthwick Castle, which was dated from 1430, with its handsome hall and stone arched roof—the earliest specimen of its kind in Scotland—had traces of both colour and decorative treatment carried out on the surface of the stone, and to the timber work of the ceiling, which dated from 1636.
In depicting the family escutcheons on these ceilings, which had now become historical, the artists had clothed them in floriated compositions of grotesque forms drawn from masks and quaint ornamental composition, and throughout the motive.
On the main feature of the schemes, were the coats-of-arms, which were painted the quarterings of many of our leading families, the medium employed being distemper.
The characteristics of the art were Scottish, and though the work of foreign artists might be observable, still they could recognise the individuality of native artists in the architecture of Falkland Palace, and Stirling Castle.
The niches above the grand gateway in the eastern side of the quadrangle of Linlithgow Palace were once occupied by statues of a Pope to represent the Church, a knight to symbolise the gentry, and a working man to symbolise the commons, each having a scroll above his head, on which were inscribed a few words of legend now irretrievably lost.
That was gathered from the records of 1535, which further showed that these statues, together with the group of the Salutation of the Virgin upon the other side of the quadrangle, and certain unicorns and a lion upon the outer gateway, were brilliantly painted.
Reference was made to three ceilings—one in the chapel of Falkland Palace and two in Holyrood Palace—which were decorated in the Heraldic style, evidently of English origin, and were probably the work of the Court official known by the name of the "Sergeant Painter"—to indicate what was reasonable as not being the work of Scotsmen, and so enable them to include the remaining specimens as purely national.
There was a growing opinion in some quarters favourable to the renewal of this mode of painting, and those impressed with the idea of introducing such an attractive style of decoration into our ecclesiastic, civic, and domestic buildings should keep in view that the character of the design should bear the impress of a native type.
Mr. Kinross and Mr. Bonnar were given hearty votes of thanks for their communications.
