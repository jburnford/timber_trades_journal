BENTLEY, SAM., joiner, Webster Lane,
Scholes.—The first meeting of the creditors
concerned hereunder took place on Friday at
the offices of the Official Receiver, Bradford.
The statement of affairs filed by the debtor
disclosed a deficiency amounting to £371 4s.
The Official Receiver stated that no resolutions
could be passed, as only one proof had been
lodged in time for voting purposes. The estate
was formally left in the hands of the Official
Receiver, for summary administration.

CAPPS, E., builder, Felixstowe. — The
adjourned public examination of this debtor
was held on Friday at the Ipswich Bankruptcy
Court before the Registrar. The summary of
accounts filed by the debtor disclosed a deficiency
of £1,010 19s. The debtor, in reply to
questions put by the Official Receiver, said he
had now filed an account showing how his deficiency
had arisen. He was questioned at considerable
length as to certain items in the
account he had filed. He said he had undervalued
certain houses at Felixstowe, but he now
found that the mortgages were satisfied with
their security, so that his deficiency would
thereby be reduced by £600. The £80 claimed
from him by Palfreyman, Foster, & Co. was in
respect of goods supplied to two houses at
Chiswick for which he was once contractor,
but he afterwards gave up the contract, and
the goods were sent subsequently. He admitted
that judgment had been obtained against him
in respect of the amount in question, but
alleged that it was because the item was
included in a bill in which items for which he
was indebted, and he had no money to put in a
defence, which would have involved his paying
the larger amount first. The debtor was
eventually allowed to pass his examination.

GREEN, JOHN FREDERICK,
timber merchant, Westbourne Villa, Ipswich.—
The public examination of the above-named
debtor came on for hearing on Friday at the
Ipswich Bankruptcy Court, before Mr. Registrar
Grimsey. The statement of affairs filed by the
bankrupt disclosed liabilities amounting to
£812 0s. 5d., and assets nil. It appeared that
there were altogether 53 unsecured creditors,
and £300 was returned as being due to
them. The debtor commenced business as a
commission agent in the timber trade about
six years ago. He had no capital of his own
at the time, but his wife lent him £200, which
he was still owing. The furniture in the house
belonged to the debtor's wife. He admitted
that most of the furniture was covered by a
bill of sale, to which he as well as his wife
was a party, and on that bill of sale
there was about £12 to pay. He alleged
his failure to have been caused through his not
having been able to do sufficient business. He
had not kept a proper set of books. He was
of opinion that he could have paid all his
creditors with the exception of his wife, two
years ago. His business consisted chiefly in
purchasing standing trees, and have them cut
into chips for the purpose of making bats. It
was a very uncertain business, as sometimes the
trees would turn out well and at other times
they would be useless. The judgment of the
most experienced man was of no avail in a
matter of that kind. The last lot, it was true,
was sold for £31, whereas they only cost him
£15. He had given a bill of sale on account of
trees he had failed to deliver. The pony and
cart belonged to his wife, although he admitted
that he had incurred expenses for repairs and
for doctoring the pony. Eventually the examination
was adjourned until the next court, and
in the meantime the debtor was ordered to
make out a statement showing all his receipts
and payments since the beginning of 1895.
