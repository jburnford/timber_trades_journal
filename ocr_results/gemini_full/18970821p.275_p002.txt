THE Uganda Railway in East Africa was visited in April last by Mr. Francis W. Fox.
He says forty-five miles of permanent way had been laid and were in good working order.
The chief engineer, Mr. Whitehouse, stated that by the end of August he hoped to have the permanent way laid for a distance of 120 miles, and that by the end of 1898 the railway would be completed to Kikuyu, a distance of about 300 miles from Mombasa.
The construction of the first forty-five miles of railway has involved heavy earthworks and bridges, and there have been many delays such as attend the initiation of such enterprises in a new country, but now that these are overcome the construction of the railway will make rapid progress.
The railway is built on the metre gauge, and there are several steep gradients of 1 in 50 in the first forty-five miles.
The special train conveyed us over fifteen miles of the railway ran at the rate of twenty miles an hour over the newly-constructed earthworks and bridges, notwithstanding that a heavy downpour of rain had occurred only a few hours before.
Owing to the recent discovery of a shorter and less costly route to Victoria Nyanza, the original length and estimated cost of the railway are likely to be considerably reduced.

ROYAL SCOTTISH ABORICULTURAL SOCIETY.
EXCURSION TO IRELAND.
A general meeting of this society was held on Monday afternoon, at 5, St. Andrew Square, Edinburgh, Mr. R. C. Munro-Ferguson, M.P., presiding.
The Chairman, in the course of his address, said that some disappointment might be felt that nothing had as yet resulted from their interview with the President of the Board of Agriculture with regard to their proposed scheme for an experimental forest area in Scotland and practical forestry school.
He could not, however, share in that disappointment, and he could say so without being reasonably suspected of entertaining any undue regard or respect either for the Department or for the Government, because no Government could give without sufficient pressure, especially in days when Government gave in proportion to the pressure which was brought to bear upon it—apparently on much the same principle as people were apt to give to the sturdiest of sturdy beggars on the street.
No one could accuse the Government of being niggardly with the public purse, and if none of the golden shower had descended on them, it was because they were unable to command sufficient support.
The society had been enthusiastic over a forest school and an experimental area, but they were a limited number of more or less trained men, an army fashioned on the long-service system, and they would find that they had to add to the ranks and create their reserves.
Working foresters had shown their bent of mind by availing themselves liberally of such instruction as Edinburgh could provide.
Some rising factors, also, despite the one-legged condition of the Chair of Forestry, had not neglected the University lectures; but since factors flourished where thistle-down blew, it might be rash to assume too much for their moderate attendance upon Colonel Bailey.
Timber merchants were well aware of what a steady supply of home-grown timber would mean for their trade.
They could get much of what they needed lying at hand instead of having to seek it abroad.
He believed that private forestry if left to itself would be generally a failure until the State took in hand both instruction in forestry and the ownership of forests.
And once State forests were started there would, he believed, be an immediate improvement in the timber management of private estates.
But their present and special business, outside of their ordinary routine, was the establishment of a forest school coupled with an experimental area.
This they advocated wholly apart from the policy of State forestry, as to which there might be divergence of opinion, whilst as to education and experiments there could be none.
To gain the requisite provision for forestry education, he pinned his faith upon this society as the best available instrument.
He believed that the whole future of forestry lay with the success of their efforts now. (Applause.)
He, for one, was confident that none of them would relax their efforts nor cease in their demands until they had secured some provision for training foresters, so that they might remove the waste and of vacillation and ignorance besetting an industry which, in its scope, might yet rank alongside of agriculture itself.
At the close of the meeting, the members set out for the annual excursion to Ireland.
The party, which numbered about eighty, left Edinburgh on Monday afternoon, a special train on the Caledonian Railway running them right on to Greenock without a stop.
At Greenock they joined the large and finely-appointed steamer, "Duke of Fife," specially placed at their disposal by the Duke Line Company.
The start from Edinburgh was made in dull, threatening weather, which, by the time the Clyde was reached, developed into a somewhat heavy drizzle of rain.
The start from Greenock was to have been made at 7.30, but it was nearer 8.30 before the ropes were flung off and the "Duke of Fife" glided out of the harbour about twenty minutes in the rear of the ordinary steamer, "Duke of Gordon,"

Both steamers had their full complement of passengers on board, but while the "Duke of Fife" carried little else besides, the "Duke of Gordon" had a substantial weight of general cargo.
The start from Greenock was made in thick, dismal rain.
Owing to the storm the ship, which should have arrived in Dublin at nine o'clock, did not get in till after twelve.
The Bay was greatly enjoyed by those who were able to be on deck, an interesting feature being added by the presence of the "Duke of Gordon," which was sighted several miles ahead off and was gradually passed in rounding Howth Head for Dublin.
On arriving at the North Wall the party were driven in brakes and "raal Irish cars" to the Gresham Hotel, which was the headquarters of the party during the week.
About two o'clock the bulk of the party set out on a drive through the famous Phoenix Park, going afterwards to the Glasnevin Royal Botanic Gardens.
The members had their annual dinner at the Gresham Hotel, when the president of the Society, Mr. R. C. Munro-Ferguson presided.
On Wednesday they visited Powerscourt, Viscount Powerscourt's beautiful seat in co. Wicklow, as their principal centre.
On Thursday they were at Coollattin and district, also in co. Wicklow; while yesterday (Friday) they visited Carton, co. Kildare, where there are a great variety of objects of interest to arborists.
A supplemental excursion has been arranged for to-day (Saturday) to Muckross, Killarney, while another for Monday and Tuesday is to Kenmare, Glengariff, Bantry, and Cork.
Many of the older members of the Society appear to be absent this year from the excursion, but there is still a very large and representative company, a gratifying feature being the presence of several English and Irish members who have taken advantage of the society's visit to Ireland to associate themselves with it.

WELLS IN ROMAN CHURCHES.
Many are the sacred wells in Roman churches whose waters the devout are eager to drink on particular festivals, and we may trace this feeling to its origin in the high efficacy to the pure element in the baptismal rite predisposing to ascribe mysterious virtues to it.
Both the reputed prisons of St. Peter and St. Paul contain such wells; another at S. Maria in Via, derives its sacredness from a Madonna picture found floating on it in the year 1253.
In the Benedictine church of St. Callisto we see, through a door beside an altar, a well of great depth and width, quite unlike those of modern formation, in which Pope Calixtus I. suffered martyrdom, being thrown from a window of the house where he had been confined and had converted a soldier, his keeper.
In the adjacent church, S. Maria in Trastevere, is kept the stone said to have been fastened to the neck of St. Calixtus when he was drowned.
Many of the species called pietra del paragone, rounded but flattened at two sides, are to be seen in Roman churches, preserved as records of martyrs; and such they may be deemed, for weights of stone used to be hung to the neck, the hands or feet of those who were to be inflicted.
It is supposed that for this purpose were employed originally serving for trade, and marked with numbers to indicate what the law allowed for scourging either weights or thongs.
Such objects when used to be dedicated to Hercules as the God of Traffic.
