GREENOCK.
A serious fire, involving the loss of life to one man and serious injury to another, occurred here early on Tuesday morning in the steam cooperage and box making establishment of Mr. John Drummond, in Dellingham Street.
The buildings and machinery are entirely destroyed and a large number of finished barrels and cases and deals in stock have been consumed.
The damage to stock is estimated at £2,500 and is fully covered by insurance.
The buildings, &c., which are the property of Mr. Stewart Allison, late sawmiller, at the same address, are only partially covered by insurance.
The joiners in this district are still in good demand, but the engineers' strike, if further protracted, will inevitably cause a slackness in this respect in the shipbuilding yards where the work has now reached an advanced stage.

THE COST OF AN INDIA EARTHQUAKE.
The official report from the secretary to the Chief Commissioner of Assam on the earthquake of June 12 last, which gives the loss of 1,542 lives and the destruction or serious quantity of property, has been received.
The cost of repairing damage to the Public Works Department alone is estimated at more than 55 lacs of rupees, or approximately over one quarter of a million pounds sterling.
These figures do not include the cost of damage to local corporation or municipaliti[es] chargeable to departmental expenditure borne by the Public Works Department.
In some instances, the Chief Commissioner states, the grant from Imperial revenues will be insufficient to recover the administration to its former efficiency for a great length of time.
"Here," writes Mr. Milne, "we have a calamity affecting the administration to its very roots, the effects of the earthquake.
Here we have a loss of life and capital which can only be remedied by the acceleration of engineering schemes.
With regard to the provision of headquarters of the district, the various headquarters of the district administration shall be shifted to more suitable sites, the interference which a deep would cause to public and private interests makes it desirable that the choice of earthquakes in the future should not be left to escape to localities where the effects might be less, but by changes in methods of construction.

GLASGOW JOINERS AND AMERICAN TIMBER GOODS.
It appears that the Glasgow Joiners' Union have withdrawn the joiners employed by several of the contractors who were using doors or window-sashes imported from America.
At a meeting of the directors of the master wrights held on Wednesday afternoon in the office of Messrs. J. L. & F L. Selkirk, West Regent Street, it was reported that the dispute had been settled and that the men had returned to work.
It is understood that the masters have agreed to the terms of the men, and that the American-made doors will not now be used.

SCOTTISH FORTRESSES.
Before the accession of James VI. to the throne of England the situation of Scotland was such that every baron's house was more or less fortified according to the power or consequence of its lord or according to the situation of the castle.
Near Edinburgh or Stirling, where the inhabitants were somewhat polished in their manners and overawed by the seat of government, no more was necessary than towers capable of resisting the cursory attacks of robbers and thieves, who never durst attempt a regular investment, but who might be plundered by surprise, and if repulsed instantly fled.
But when further removed, as in Perthshire, Inverness-shire, or Aberdeenshire, then it was necessary to be better defended; and the aids of a peel or dungeon, with outer walls, moat, wet ditch and barnakin were added to enable the lord of the keep to resist the formidable attack of his powerful adversary.
The history of Scotland, so late as the reign of the Stuart family, affords a number of melancholy instances of inveterate feuds among the greater and lesser barons of that period, and in every mode of fortification then in use was seldom adequate to the defence of the castle against the storm or blockade of the enraged chieftain.
The third kind of fortress which we meet with in Scotland consists of those situated on the borders of England, or on the sea-coasts of the kingdom, and in the Western Isles and in very remote places.
Many of the old castles in Scotland were situated on an island in a deep lake, or on a peninsula which by a broad deep cut was made an island.
This was only accessible in a hard frost or by boats, which were not easily transported by land, and when ported by a people destitute of good roads and wheel carriages.
In fact, they could only be taken by surprise or blockade, the first of which was difficult and the second tedious, and the use of artillery they might be deemed almost impregnable.
