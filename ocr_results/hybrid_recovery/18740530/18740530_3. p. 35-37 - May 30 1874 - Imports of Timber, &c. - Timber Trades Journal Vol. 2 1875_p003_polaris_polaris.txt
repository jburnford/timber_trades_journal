Net Imports.

Railway Sleepers.—This market appears once more well supplied, and this without any excessive stock being held at present afloat, although large arrivals are expected during the first half of the present month.
The shipments already effected amount to a considerable quantity, and if fair consumption is maintained there should be no danger of prices falling, if purchasers would only fight shy of so much competition in the matter of buying at the joining ports of despatch, and so permit prices to fall and purchases be effected at the ports of shipment on a basis which would pay better in the end.
The consumption and import of creosoted timber is extending rapidly.
It is to this branch of the trade we must now almost entirely look for any improvement in the business.
Home supplies of home-grown sleepers will in future be available and must not be overlooked, considering also that in the meanwhile the competition of foreign timber is daily growing keener.
Scotland appears to have been well supplied from Ilamburgh and the Black Sea with beech and oak-tree sleepers, and are moreover drawing from this country.
In all probability timber for creosoting will be principally drawn from Russia and the North of Europe.

DECREASE OF WOOD.—In the report of the United States Department of Agriculture for the year 1870, on the statement is made that such is the wholesale destruction of American forests, there will be much scant material for wood to the country within thirty years, unless immediate measures are taken to supply their places by new plantations.
It is estimated that from 1850 to 1860, 20,000,000 acres of timber land were brought under cultivation, and in the present decade no less than 100,000,000 will be reclaimed.
THE CANADIAN TIMBER DUES.—The Special Committee of the Dominion Parliament charged with inquiring into the working of the new duty on logs and shingle supplies and sales to both houses the report that the country, although the saw-mill, has not has been slightly benefited by of the duty, that it has been at the expense of settlers and owners of timber, and, instead of cheapening the cost of lumber, has in many localities especially at the Lake Erie ports, produced the Miqualture.
It is claimed, that the duty has almost a tax upon Michigan timber, and driven a large amount of capital from Canada into the pineers of Michigan and Wisconsin.
The Committee recommend that the law so modified that the burdens imposed be more equally distributed, and a bill will be introduced to that end. — Oswego Palladium.
