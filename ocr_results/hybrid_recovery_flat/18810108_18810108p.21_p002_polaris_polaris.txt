                    AMERICAN AND CANADIAN
                             ITEMS.—(Continued.)

      The figures relating to the lumber trade of the United States also show a
satisfactory increase, although not so marked as in the Dominion, and there is every
prospect of an active demand during the coming season.

      The square timber is now cut on the Ottawa chiefly at a minimum standard of
12x12.

      At the New Brunswick camps the logs now being squared are much larger than those
of previous seasons, the operations having been pushed further back into the forest
regions.

      Owing to the scarcity of labour on the river, wages have ruled high, and the
cost of production has been affected accordingly.

      The Dresden Board of Trade, in their recent report, call attention to the
steady increase in the German import of foreign timber.

      They, however, add that the home forests must not be neglected.

      The American lumber combinations, though powerful, have not yet succeeded in
obtaining control of the whole market.

      A new law in one of the Western States imposes penalties on the wasteful
destruction of young timber.

      Recent advices from California state that the redwood output for the past season
has been unusually large.

      The latest Chicago figures also point to an active movement in all descriptions
of timber, both for building and for export purposes.

      The Australian timber trade likewise shows signs of improvement.

      In conclusion, it may be stated that the general condition of the timber trade
throughout the world is regarded as hopeful by leading authorities.
