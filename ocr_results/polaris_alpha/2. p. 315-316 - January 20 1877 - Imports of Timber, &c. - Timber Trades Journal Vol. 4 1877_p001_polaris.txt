January 13, 1877.]

THE TIMBER TRADES JOURNAL.

Important Legal Decisions.

At the Brecon County Court, on the 11th inst., the Judge (F. T. James, Esq.), County Court Judge of the circuit, had before him an important case to the trade, in which Mr. R. Jones, of Merthyr, was plaintiff, and Mr. W. B. Davies, of Swansea, defendant.  
The plaintiff claimed £43 18s. for sawn timber, shipped at Quebec for Swansea, in September, 1876.  
The main question was, whether the terms of the contract had been fulfilled.  
The evidence, on both sides, was extremely conflicting.  
His Honour, in summing up, said he had to be guided by the general law, and according to the custom of this port, the contract was not fulfilled; the buyer was entitled to “fair merchantable timber,” — that is, timber that was suitable for the market.  
The truth was, there was a great discrepancy in the evidence; but he was bound to give the plaintiff a verdict for £30.  
The plaintiff was also allowed costs on the amount recovered.  
At the conclusion of the case, His Honour made some important observations with regard to such contracts which we have been kindly permitted to publish in an abridged form.  
The learned Judge said the case had been argued according to the custom of Swansea, but no custom had been proved.  
Now, as to custom, custom, if proved, undoubtedly is binding.  
The law as to custom is often misunderstood.  
Custom is binding, but not necessarily in the place where it exists.  
For instance, if a custom were proved to exist at Swansea that a certain class of timber imported there should be paid for in a particular way, it would not be binding upon buyers and sellers in that port only, but all over the kingdom.  
But it must be shown that such custom has existed for a reasonable time, and that it has been uniformly adopted and acquiesced in, and that it has not been in opposition to the general law of the land.  
On the other hand, a usage of trade, different from the general law, is binding only in the place where it exists, and then only when it is consistent with the general law.  
He had heard a great deal of evidence which was said to be as to custom, but he felt bound to say that nothing like legal evidence of custom had been given, and that no custom had been proved to exist in Swansea upon the subject.  
He must, therefore, decide the case upon general principles.  
It was said that the plaintiff was bound to take the whole cargo.  
That was not the law, and the plaintiff was not bound to take the whole cargo; he was only bound to take such part as came up to the terms of the contract.  
The other side had said that the buyer was bound to take the whole cargo, but that he might reject bad pieces.  
That might be the mode in which a contract was made, but in the absence of proof of custom it was not the rule of law.  
He thought that in this case the buyer was entitled to reject such pieces as were not according to contract.  
The jury had found that the timber was not what is called “fair merchantable timber,” and that therefore the defendant was entitled to reject it.  
He thought that the verdict was a very proper one.  
He was very much struck with the evidence given by one of the witnesses, who said that although the timber was “sappy” and inferior, still it was such as was usually imported at Swansea.  
If so, it proved too much.  
It is not because inferior timber is frequently imported into a port that therefore the buyer is bound to take it.  
He thought that the law upon the subject was very clear, and that the defendant was justified in rejecting the timber.  
Under these circumstances he felt bound to give a verdict for the defendant for £30, leaving the plaintiff to pay his own costs.  
This decision is of great importance to the timber trade generally, as it shows that in the absence of a proved usage, it is not sufficient for a seller to show that the article sent is such as is usually imported at the port of delivery, but he must also show that it comes up to the description contained in the contract.  
We trust that this decision will have the effect of making buyers more careful in seeing that the terms of their contracts are strictly adhered to, and that sellers will also be more particular in seeing that the quality of the article they supply is in accordance with the terms of the contract.  
It may be as well to add that His Honour remarked that if there had been a proved custom that the buyer should take the cargo, rejecting only such pieces as were absolutely bad, he should have felt bound to act upon it; but no such custom had been proved, and the case must therefore be decided upon general principles.
